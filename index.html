Our Mission Statement

Ultimately, students’ distinct worldview is their greatest leverage for creating agency and active citizenship.

School is built on a thesis of building mastery - but we don’t often question how our lived experiences, unique problem solving capabilities and identities shape real world impact.

Problem solving involves an intersection of all of those perspectives in creating a more accessible and sustainable world.

We exist to empower every student to explore that potential on a national scale, putting student voice and choice first.

The Petri Dish of Innovation

1

Your story is data

Every student carries knowledge no textbook has: what it’s like to live with a condition, a barrier, or a gap in their community. We treat that experience as evidence worth investigating. It’s where every problem in the sprint begins.

2

Curiosity is the method

Students seek out perspectives, identities, and experiences that differ from their own, and every new one adds a dimension the problem didn’t have before. Curiosity shapes how they approach open problems and teaches them to build with others, not for them.

3

The real world is the rubric

Collaborating directly with real-world stakeholders, students realize that budgets and timelines aren't just limitations—they are the parameters of reality. They learn that leadership is the bridge between a good idea and a real-world reality.

Same Process, Different Problems

Amina, 16 · with two peers

The Elders’ Archive

Realizing their grandparents were the last speakers of their local dialects, 16-year-old Amina and two peers partnered with the public library to build a permanent audio archive. By negotiating strict consent and access rules directly with the elders, the students delivered a community-controlled historical collection.

Nia, 16 · cross-school team

The Youth Seat

Realizing her town’s recreation committee made youth decisions without youth input, 16-year-old Nia and her cross-school team pitched data-backed interviews to the committee chair. The students successfully negotiated the creation of a permanent youth observer seat shared by their schools.

Ines, 15

The Open Creek Kit

Ines, 15 grew up beside a small creek that no one tests, and a local watershed group had no staff to sample it. She designed a low-cost sensor kit that any volunteer can build and use, uploading readings to a shared map. A partner school’s lab checked the kit against standard methods and published its limits, and the group now uses it across the creek.

The Foundation

Leadership and Storytelling

Students learn to turn their own experience into a reason to act.

Theoretical frameworkWe build on Marshall Ganz’s storytelling method from Harvard Kennedy School, widely used to train community organizers, and Kouzes and Posner’s leadership research, which is taught in leadership programs around the world.

Community Research and Design

Students investigate real problems alongside the people who live with them, then compare their options using evidence.

Theoretical frameworkStanford’s d.school approach to design is used by companies and schools alike, and the Pugh chart is a standard decision tool in engineering.

Project-Based Learning

Students spend weeks on a real problem, then present the result publicly.

Theoretical frameworkEvery project starts from the end goal using backward design, which educators worldwide have used since 1998. We also design for every learner with Universal Design for Learning, now defined and endorsed in U.S. federal education law.

Blended into one student-led, real-world experience, using methods educators and professionals already rely on.

Our Process

Eight weeks, from your own story to something someone else can build on.

From Experience to Insight

Week 1

Storytelling and self-awareness

Students create a visual of a barrier they’ve faced and tell its story in three beats: the challenge, the choice they made, and what happened.

Learning outcomeThe confidence to turn a personal struggle into a problem worth solving.
Week 2

Problem analysis

Students keep asking “why?” until they reach the underlying cause, then map who in their town is already working on it, and research the blindspots.

Learning outcomeThe skill of identifying a gap instead of solving a symptom.

Listen First, Find a Partner

Week 3

Stakeholder research

Students interview three stakeholders from their Week 2 map to see what’s been tried, what’s working and what’s missing.

Learning outcomeThe skill of asking the right questions when answers are one search away, and of seeing what local knowledge can tell you that a textbook can’t.
Week 4

Partnership and communication

Students approach a local organization, share what they’ve learned, and ask “what do you need?”

Learning outcomeThe judgment to build with a partner, not for them.

The result is a real partner who agrees to co-own the project.

Define Success, Then Build

Week 5

Goal setting and accountability

With their partner, students define what a solution must achieve and do before building anything, then set their measures of success against real standards.

Learning outcomeThe skill of measuring real impact, so students can show what changed and for whom.
Week 6

Design and prototyping

Students generate several ideas and put each through comparison tools like decision matrices, scoring every option against the goals they set with their partner.

Learning outcomeThe skill of making a choice they can explain and back up.

Take Feedback, Then Improve

Week 7

Feedback and revision

Students put their first version in front of others and learn that honest feedback is how work improves. Every comment has to be kind, specific, and helpful.

Learning outcomeThe courage to share unfinished work and the skill of improving it.

Their partner then checks it against the success measures from Week 5, and students revise into a stronger second version, with a change log showing what improved and why.

Publish It and Pass It On

Week 8 · Publish

A record someone can build on

Students publish a clear record of their project and their reasoning, with a jumping-off plan for what comes next, so another student can start from their work instead of from zero.

Learning outcomeThe skill of making their thinking visible and useful to others.
Week 8 · Present

Public showcase

Students present to their partner, families and community at a public showcase, streamed live and recorded.

Learning outcomePublic speaking and the poise to think on their feet when someone asks a hard question.

FAQs

What to know before your first cohort.

How long is a sprint?

Eight weeks: find a problem, find a partner, define success and build, revise, then publish and present.

When does the first cohort start?

January 2027. Cohorts run in eight-week blocks, like January–February and March–April. We’re gathering interest now so we can prepare the materials and schedules in advance.

How much time does a team need?

Teams should plan on 5-7 hours a week.

How self-directed is it?

Mostly. Each week has a clear task, and biweekly office hours and checkpoint reviews help teams stay on track.

What do founders need to do?

Our program is very hands-off for founders. Suggest teams a regular time to meet, like after school, or during school in your prep time. We handle the materials, stakeholder help, and office hours.

What do founders get for hosting?

Your school receives $20 for every student who takes part, sponsored-seat students included. It’s paid in cash the week after the cohort ends and goes back into your school. Use the calculator in Contact and Apply to see your total.

Can we make it our own?

Yes. Add your own twist and have students connect the sprint to a subject they’re already studying, or a learning goal you wanted to build a project on.

Do students need prior experience?

No. The first two weeks start from what each student already knows.

How does it end?

In Week 8, teams publish a project record and a jumping-off plan, then present at a public showcase, streamed live and recorded.

What’s a sponsored seat?

The same full sprint, with the cost covered. Each cohort has two, awarded by application. Founders apply on behalf of the student and review the application with them, and the student then takes part just like everyone else.

Is the price per student or per school?

Per student, so a team of four is $600 USD. Every team gets the same pooled project budget, whatever its size.

Can we start free and upgrade later?

Yes. Teams that finish Weeks 1–2 can join a cohort for Week 3 onward.
